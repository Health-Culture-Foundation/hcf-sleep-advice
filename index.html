The short answer

We read sleep medicine guidelines from the US, UK, Europe and its national bodies, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Latin America, Japan, China, South Korea, India and WHO. For each one we noted what it recommends, how strongly, how old it is, who wrote it, and what payers and care routes actually require.

Everyone agrees on one thing

A short course of talking therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) should come first. Every guideline we found says so.

Almost nothing else matches

Which sleeping pills to use, for how long, whether melatonin is a supplement or a prescription drug, and how strict payers are about CPAP use all vary widely.

Nobody has compared it properly

We found no thorough, current, open comparison of sleep guidelines across countries down to individual drugs.

Care routes are an even bigger gap

Who you see, in what order, and who pays has barely been compared at all, especially for insomnia.

Much of it is old

The main US drug guideline is from 2017. France's insomnia guidance is from 2006. Canada's sleep apnea guideline is from 2011.

Some of it is closed

The UK's main GP guide is publicly funded but privately owned, readable only from inside the UK, and may not be mined.

Insomnia: what each country says

"CBT-I" is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia: a few sessions, in person or by app, that change sleep habits and thoughts about sleep. "Z-drugs" are zolpidem, zopiclone, eszopiclone and zaleplon. "Orexin drugs" are the newest sleeping pills: suvorexant, lemborexant and daridorexant. A dash means the guideline we read didn't address it, or we didn't capture it.

Same drug, opposite advice

  • Trazodone: the US (AASM, VA/DoD) suggests against it. Korea and Brazil support it. Europe allows low-dose antidepressants short-term; the Netherlands advises against them.
  • Orexin drugs: the US VA/DoD, Brazil and China rank them highly. The UK allows daridorexant only if CBT-I failed or isn't available. Canada approved two of them and then advised public plans not to pay for either.
  • Benzodiazepines: AASM weakly supports temazepam and triazolam. VA/DoD and Brazil advise against benzodiazepines.

Melatonin: five different rules

  • Supplement anyone can buy: US, Canada.
  • Pharmacy product for adults: New Zealand (since Oct 2025); Australia for 55+.
  • Prescription medicine: UK, France (above supplement doses), Japan (children only).
  • Tiny supplement dose only: Brazil, capped at 0.21 mg a day.
  • Banned in supplements: Denmark.

Sleep apnea: testing, CPAP and the new weight-loss drug

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) means breathing stops and starts during sleep. "AHI" is the number of pauses per hour. Diagnosis is either an overnight lab study or a simpler home test. The main treatment is CPAP, a mask that keeps the airway open. In December 2024 the US approved tirzepatide (Zepbound), a weight-loss drug, for OSA in people with obesity.

Other conditions

Restless legs: the sharpest split

In 2025 the US (AASM) moved to suggest against routine use of dopamine agonists such as pramipexole and ropinirole, because over time they can make symptoms worse. It now prefers gabapentin-type drugs and IV iron. Europe's only formal guideline, from 2012, still gives dopamine agonists its top rating. The World Sleep Society backed the US guideline only "with caveats" because of "regional disagreement". Italy (2026) has switched to the US position.

Narcolepsy: guidelines agree, payers don't

The US and European 2021 guidelines both strongly recommend modafinil, pitolisant, sodium oxybate and solriamfetol. England pays for solriamfetol only after modafinil and then a stimulant have failed. The EU has limited modafinil to narcolepsy since 2010.

Melatonin for children

A supplement in the US (sleep doctors say see a clinician first). Prescription-only licensed products in the EU, Australia and Japan. Denmark: it "should never be the first choice" in otherwise healthy children.

Children's sleep apnea

China won't diagnose it without a lab study. Canada (2026) is the first to accept home tests, for healthy children over 5 where lab studies aren't available. France (2023) needs no sleep study if a child has two or more night-time symptoms.

Pathways and payers: what actually happens

A guideline says what to do. The care route decides who does it and in what order, and the payer decides what gets paid for. Often the payer has the last word.

Payers overrule guidelines

Germany's guideline makes no long-term call on orexin drugs, but the payer covers daridorexant past 4 weeks. The same guideline calls mouth splints first-line, but the payer pays only after CPAP fails.

Approved but not paid for

Canada approved two orexin drugs and then advised public plans not to fund either. Australia doesn't subsidise any of them.

CPAP use rules differ 4-fold

US and Korea: 4+ hours on 70% of nights. France: 112 hours per 28 days. Japan: payment stops only below 1 hour a day. England and Ontario: no use rule for funding.

Who pays for insomnia therapy?

  • Germany pays for three insomnia apps on prescription (about €225–241, no co-payment).
  • Scotland links people straight to free digital CBT-I, no GP visit needed. In England it depends on the local NHS commissioner.
  • Australia counts insomnia toward up to 10 subsidised psychology sessions a year.
  • US Medicare pays for approved therapy apps only alongside clinician-led care. The VA has trained its own therapists in CBT-I since about 2011–12.
  • Canada: no public coverage found.
  • Netherlands: guided online CBT-I is one step in a "lightest suitable form first" model.

Who you see first for sleep apnea

In the Netherlands, the GP refers on and does no testing. In Australia, the GP screens and a specialist can approve a home test without seeing the patient. In the US, any treating clinician can order a test and CPAP, and an equipment supplier delivers it. Only Canada sets a waiting-time standard for sleep care.

Has anyone compared this before?

Not thoroughly.Nothing published covers several countries, several conditions and individual drugs, with care routes and payer rules, kept current and open as data. Care routes are the biggest gap.

Who owns the guidance doctors actually use

When we tried to read the UK's main GP guide to insomnia, NICE's Clinical Knowledge Summaries (CKS), we got a block page. It says CKS "is not NICE content and is excluded from the NICE UK Open Content Licence", that it is readable only from the UK, and that "data scraping and data mining of CKS is not permitted". So we didn't try to get around it. We looked at who owns it instead.

  • CKS began as PRODIGY, an NHS-funded project at Newcastle University in the 1990s.
  • NICE has paid Clarity Informatics to write it since 2012. The last contract was about £1.32 million for April 2023 to April 2026, and only one bid was received.
  • Clarity has been owned since 2021 by Agilio Software, a UK healthcare software group. Agilio was sold in 2024 to funds advised by Five Arrows (Rothschild & Co) and TA Associates.
  • Agilio states: "The content within CKS is the intellectual property of Agilio." Outside the UK the same content is sold by subscription. Its topic list includes insomnia, sleep apnea and restless legs.

The UK's drug reference, the BNF, works the same way. Three professional bodies own it, and NICE pays £15.16 million over 2021–2033 for UK access, awarded without competition "due to IPR ownership".

It's a pattern

Most of the guidance doctors use day to day is paid or login-only: UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, DynaMed, Australia's Therapeutic Guidelines, HealthPathways. Some governments buy national licences. That makes the content free to use in that country, but never open, and it can disappear. Norway dropped BMJ Best Practice on 1 April 2026 and deleted its translated patient leaflets the same day. Society guidelines are more often free to read (Netherlands, Germany, Japan, NICE's own guidelines), but machine reuse is increasingly restricted. Germany's guideline register forbids text mining and AI use. NICE's own licence covers the UK only and excludes AI use.

The public pays twice.Once for the research and the experts, and again to license the summary back. And people outside the paying country can't see it at all. We found studies on paywalled specialist guidelines, but none on how closed GP guidance is. That is another gap.

Is there a standard format for this?

Yes, several, but none was built for comparing countries.

  • WHO SMART Guidelines sets out steps from a written guideline to working software. There are 10 kits so far (antenatal care, HIV, TB, immunisation and others). None covers sleep or mental health.
  • HL7 FHIR, the main modern health-data standard, has a guideline format with slots for a recommendation's direction, strength and evidence quality, plus a "pathway" type. Its newest recommendation profiles are still drafts.
  • GRADE gives the words most guideline panels use: for or against, strong or conditional, and certainty of evidence from high to very low.
  • Care routes have clear definitions and can be drawn as flowcharts, but there is no shared data format. Map of Medicine, a commercial pathway library, closed in 2018 and its content went with it.
  • Payer rules have no format that works across countries.

So the dataset stays plain JSON, with optional fields named after GRADE and FHIR terms, ICD-11 condition codes and ATC drug codes. It can be exported to FHIR later if a partner needs it.

Caveats

What we couldn't read

  • NICE CKS and the BNF: UK-only, mining banned.
  • Germany's 2025 insomnia guideline PDF: its register forbids text mining and AI use, so those rows rest on a pharmacy-press summary. A person should copy the grades.
  • Finland's national guideline: the site was down.
  • Australian HealthPathways: login only.
  • Japan's 2013 sleeping-pill guideline PDF: connection refused.
  • Many papers: abstract only. Each row says what was read.

Numbers to check before quoting

  • Japan's 2026 CPAP cut-offs (AHI 15/30 vs 20/40), read from a commercial copy.
  • France's 112 hours per 28 days, and its proposed 2026 changes.
  • Germany's app prices (€224.99–€241.25).
  • England 2017–18: 1.4 million adults on benzodiazepines, 1.0 million on Z-drugs.
  • Australia's questionnaire cut-off (STOP-Bang ≥3).
  • CKS contract value (two official notices differ: £1,320,000 vs £1,323,000).

Conflicts of interest worth knowing

  • Brazil 2023: many drug-company ties on the panel, including the Latin American partner for daridorexant.
  • Japan 2020 sleep apnea guideline: heavy ties to CPAP makers.
  • US restless legs 2025: the chair consulted for the maker of the IV iron the guideline strongly recommends.
  • The tirzepatide sleep apnea trials were funded by the drug's maker, Lilly.
  • UK BAP 2019 declares no conflicts, but a co-author is listed on PubMed with the company that makes Sleepio.

How this was made

Nine AI research agents worked in parallel on 21 October 2026, one per region or topic, under fixed rules: no citation without a PubMed ID or DOI that was looked up, or a page that was read; numbers copied exactly; dates, status and conflicts of interest recorded; blocks and licence terms respected. All 155 PubMed IDs were re-checked at the end. This is a first pass and needs review by a person.
