# Mind and behaviour: literature search (2026-10-21)

Area: psychological and behavioural techniques. 53 findings from 46 distinct sources are in `mind-behavior.json`. Some sources appear more than once, mapped to different concepts. Almost everything was read as the **abstract only**. Every PMID and DOI was looked up in PubMed (E-utilities) or OpenAlex, and open access was checked in OpenAlex.

## What I searched
PubMed (E-utilities, sorted by relevance) for:
- CBT-I guidelines and the component network meta-analysis, plus stimulus control, sleep restriction, paradoxical intention, ACT and digital or self-help CBT-I
- countercontrol, time in bed and quiet wakefulness, and adherence to stimulus control
- slow breathing, 4-7-8, cyclic sighing and box breathing, PMR, autogenic training, mindfulness, yoga nidra / NSDR and meditation apps
- to-do lists, constructive worry, worry postponement, imagery distraction and cognitive shuffling, clock monitoring, gratitude and self-compassion
- the consensus sleep diary, binaural beats, pink noise, ASMR, nature sounds and music

I also searched OpenAlex for Beaudoin's cognitive shuffle work. For wording I checked the NHLBI insomnia pages, which are free government pages.

## Strongest findings by concept
- **cbt-for-insomnia (5).** CBT-I is the first-line treatment in three guidelines:
  - AASM 2021 (strong), PMID 33164742
  - ACP 2016 (strong), PMID 27136449
  - European 2023 (grade A, in-person or digital), PMID 38016484

  The new AASM 2026 combination guideline (PMID 41975142) suggests CBT-I alone rather than CBT-I plus medication. Digital CBT-I works: in Soh 2020 the ISI fell by -5.00 versus control. Remission is higher with a therapist or supported program than with fully automated apps, and in the automated apps only 58.4% of people completed all sessions (Takano 2025). Sleep hygiene tips alone are clearly weaker than CBT-I (Chung 2018; AASM suggests against using them as a single therapy).
- **sleep-restriction-therapy (5).** The HABIT trial (Lancet 2023, n = 642) had nurses deliver it in GP practices. ISI was -3.05 versus a sleep hygiene booklet, it was cost-effective, and no serious adverse events were linked to it. This supports the "with a clinician's help" wording. The component NMA gives an iOR of 1.49.
- **get-up-if-you-cant-sleep / bed-is-for-sleep / stimulus-control (4).** AASM gives it a conditional recommendation as a single therapy. In the component NMA (Furukawa 2024, 241 trials) the iOR is 1.43 (CI 1.00-2.05), which is borderline. Its 2024 meta-analysis beat passive controls (g = 0.38-0.85) but was about equal to other active treatments. In older adults it was as good as sleep restriction (Epstein 2012). None of these abstracts states the "20 minutes" figure; NHLBI says only "getting out of bed if you cannot sleep".
- **progressive-muscle-relaxation (3).** Two 2026 meta-analyses show large effects on sleep quality (SMD -1.74; g = -1.24), but heterogeneity is very high, effects were uncertain in people 55 and over, and the samples were mostly patient groups.
- **relaxation-techniques (3, mixed).** AASM conditionally recommends relaxation on its own, but in the component NMA relaxation was "potentially counterproductive" inside CBT-I (iOR 0.81, CI crosses 1). Mindfulness beats attention-matched controls (ES 0.33) but not established sleep treatments (Rusch 2019).
- **try-a-guided-meditation (2-3).** The evidence is for mindfulness *courses* (Black 2015 in older adults; Wang 2020 meta-analysis of 5 RCTs) and small yoga nidra trials. I found no good review of consumer meditation apps or single bedtime recordings.
- **write-down-worries (3).** Scullin 2018: writing a specific to-do list for 5 minutes led to faster sleep onset than writing about completed tasks, on polysomnography (n = 57, one night). Constructive worry added benefit to behaviour therapy in a 22-person feasibility trial. Worry postponement reduced worry but **did not improve sleep** (McCarrick 2025, n = 186).
- **dont-check-the-clock (3).** Tang 2007: experimental clock monitoring increased worry and sleep onset latency in both good sleepers and people with insomnia.
- **keep-a-sleep-diary (4).** The Consensus Sleep Diary (PMC free) and the European guideline (grade A for diaries in assessment).

## New advice found
- **NEW: stop trying hard to sleep (paradoxical intention / sleep effort).**
  - Paradoxical intention meta-analysis (Jansson-Fröjmark 2022): large effects versus passive controls and moderate effects versus active ones.
  - ACT meta-analysis (2026): not significantly different from CBT-I on insomnia severity, though CBT-I had better remission.
  - Theory: Espie's attention-intention-effort pathway.
  - Suggested line: "If trying hard to fall asleep keeps you awake, stop trying: let sleep come on its own."
- **NEW: calm music at bedtime.** A Cochrane review (2022, 13 RCTs) found moderate-certainty evidence of better PSQI sleep quality (MD -2.79). This is the best evidence in the whole "sounds" group. There is currently no music concept.

## Key disagreement: get up (NHLBI / stimulus control) vs stay in bed and breathe (Breus)
- **Evidence for getting up.** It is a recommended, measurable CBT-I component (AASM conditional; NMA iOR 1.43), but the benefit over other active treatments is small and the trials are old.
- **Evidence for staying in bed.** I found no trial showing that lying in bed awake and resting is restorative or helps insomnia. A cross-sectional study (Birling 2020) links staying in bed awake with insomnia severity, but cannot show cause.
- **Middle ground: "countercontrol".** This means staying in bed but stopping the attempt to sleep: sitting up and doing something calm. One small 1986 trial (Davies, n = 34) found it cut sleep-maintenance complaints by about 30%. Zwart & Lisman 1979 (PMID 429643) compared stimulus control components including countercontrol, but has no PubMed abstract, so I did not report its result. Acceptance-based therapy and paradoxical intention also support "don't fight wakefulness".
- **Practical point.** Adherence to getting up is limited by boredom and annoyance (Vincent 2008). Advice to have a calm, pleasant out-of-bed activity ready is sensible.
- **Suggested reconciliation for the dataset.** Keep "get up after about 20 minutes" as the evidence-based default. Add that if getting up is not possible, the next best option is to stop trying to sleep (sit up and read in dim light, or breathe slowly) rather than lie there trying. Don't claim that resting awake replaces sleep. The **"rest-even-if-awake" plain line is not supported** as worded.

## Other disagreements and caveats
- **Breathing.** Cyclic sighing (Balban 2023) improved mood and respiratory rate more than meditation, but **measured no sleep outcomes**. Its senior author, Huberman, popularised the technique.
- **4-7-8.** Only an acute heart-rate-variability and blood-pressure study (Vierra 2022), with no sleep outcome.
- **Slow breathing overall.** The 2026 Sleep Med Rev systematic review (9 studies) found better self-reported sleep but inconclusive objective sleep. The current concept evidence levels of 3 for 4-7-8 look generous.
- **Pink noise.** In Capezuti 2022, 9 of 11 pink-noise studies were positive, but there was "no strong evidence", and many studies are closed-loop stimulation rather than a steady background sound. This overlaps with the environment area.
- **Binaural beats.** The best-controlled trial (double-blind, n = 43) found no difference from music alone. The current plain line ("evidence for sleep is mixed") is fair.
- **Gratitude and self-compassion.** Small trials, correlational data and one research group meta-analysing its own work; evidence 2.
- **Conflicts of interest to check in full text:** Espie (Big Health / Sleepio) on the dCBT-I and HABIT trials, and Huberman on Balban.

## Gaps (nothing good found)
- **cognitive-shuffling.** Beaudoin's serial diverse imagining results exist only as conference abstracts. SFU Summit 2016 (no DOI; n = 154 students, as effective as structured problem-solving) is OA at http://summit.sfu.ca/item/16196. Beaudoin's company, CogSci Apps, makes the app. These are **not in the JSON** because they have no PMID or DOI for the results. The JSON uses Harvey & Payne 2002 (imagery distraction) and Lemyre 2020 as proxies.
- **stay-in-bed-and-breathe / rest-even-if-awake.** There is no outcome evidence for quiet wakefulness in bed. The only PubMed research on quiet wakefulness concerns memory consolidation, not insomnia.
- **nature-sounds-or-asmr.** No reviews. ASMR studies are about brain activity or viewing, not sleep outcomes.
- **Meditation apps.** Only small pilot RCTs (Calm, students), so not included.
- **yoga nidra / NSDR.** One small systematic review with high risk of bias; NSDR studies cover daytime performance only.
- **get-help-if-poor-sleep-persists.** There is no trial-type evidence. The NHLBI insomnia page (free government page, not in the JSON because it has no PMID or DOI) defines chronic insomnia as 3 or more nights a week for more than 3 months and says providers may ask for a sleep diary. The guidelines (AASM, ACP, European) support seeing a clinician for chronic insomnia, since CBT-I is first-line.
- **autogenic training.** Only a pilot in athletes; not included.
