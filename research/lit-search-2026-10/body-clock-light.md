# Body clock, light and timing: literature search (2026-10-21)

Findings are in `body-clock-light.json` (40 findings). Everything there was looked up by PMID or DOI in PubMed (NCBI E-utilities). Open access was checked against PMC and OpenAlex. Most entries are based on the abstract only. The full text was read for Brown 2022 (PLoS Biol) and for the DSWPD recommendations in AASM 2015.

## What I searched
PubMed searches covered: sleep regularity, social jetlag, weekend catch-up sleep, morning and daytime light, light at night, evening room light and melatonin, e-readers and smartphones, Night Shift and screen filters, blue-blocking and amber glasses, red light at night, light therapy and melatonin for DSWPD, the AASM circadian guidelines, Cochrane reviews on melatonin for jet lag and on shift work (with and without drugs), pre-flight phase advance, nap meta-analyses (cognition, mortality, night shift) and night-owl/chronotype trials.

## Strongest findings by concept
- **keep-a-regular-sleep-schedule**: well supported. The NSF 2023 consensus (PMID 37684151) states that "consistency of sleep onset and offset timing is important for health, safety, and performance." A 2025 systematic review (59 studies) and a UK Biobank cohort agree: the least regular sleepers had 20-48% higher all-cause mortality than more regular quintiles, and regularity predicted mortality better than sleep duration. All the evidence is observational, and there are no intervention trials yet. Suggested support: 4-5.
- **get-up-at-the-same-time**: there is no separate evidence for wake time alone. The NSF consensus covers onset and offset together, so this concept can cite the regularity sources.
- **dont-sleep-in-much-on-weekends / myth-weekend-catch-up**: the sources disagree (see below). Social jetlag meta-analyses find small links to higher BMI, waist circumference and HbA1c, mostly from cross-sectional data. In an RCT (Depner 2019), weekend recovery sleep did not prevent loss of insulin sensitivity. A device-measured cohort (Chaput 2024, n=73 513) found weekend catch-up sleep was not associated with mortality or CVD. I found no evidence for the specific "within about an hour" threshold.
- **get-morning-daylight**: the principle is well supported, but the "soon after waking" detail rests on mechanistic studies. Brown 2022 consensus targets are: at least 250 lux melanopic EDI during the day, no more than 10 lux in the 3 h before sleep, and no more than 1 lux while sleeping. In UK Biobank, more outdoor daylight was linked to easier waking and fewer insomnia symptoms, and brighter days to lower mortality. In a lab study (Burgess 2003), morning bright light after waking advanced the clock 2.1 h in 3 days, compared with 0.6 h in dim light.
- **less-bright-light-in-the-evening**: strong mechanistic support. In Gooley 2011, room light (<200 lux) before bed delayed melatonin onset in 99.0% of people and shortened melatonin duration by about 90 min. In Phillips 2019, half-maximal melatonin suppression occurred at 24.60 lux, and people varied more than 10-fold in sensitivity. That dose-response indirectly supports **dim-lights-gradually**, which has no direct study.
- **screens-before-bed (the light mechanism)**: experiments that keep the content the same find real but modest effects of screen light. E-readers delayed melatonin and sleep onset compared with printed books (Chang 2015). Reading on a smartphone reduced early deep sleep in young adults (Höhn 2024). In an RCT of 167 young adults (Duraccio 2021), iPhone Night Shift made no difference to sleep. This fits Hartstein 2024 and Bauducco 2024: light is one mechanism, and content and lost sleep time also matter.
- **blue-blocking-glasses**: the evidence is weak and mixed. The 2023 Cochrane review rates the effect on sleep as very low certainty, with 6 RCTs split 3 positive and 3 null. A 2025 meta-analysis of 3 RCTs found no significant actigraphy effects. A 2020 meta-analysis found small-to-medium benefits, mostly in clinical groups (insomnia, bipolar disorder, DSPS, ADHD). Suggested support: 2.
- **light-therapy-for-a-late-body-clock**: AASM 2015 makes **no recommendation** for light therapy alone in adults with DSWPD because evidence is insufficient. It gives a WEAK FOR recommendation for light plus behavioural treatment in children and adolescents, and a WEAK FOR recommendation for **timed melatonin in adults**. A meta-analysis found light therapy helps circadian rhythm disorders (g = 0.41), with smaller effects in RCTs.
- **melatonin-for-jet-lag-or-shifts**: supported. Cochrane on jet lag: melatonin at the destination bedtime helps on flights crossing 5 or more time zones, with an NNT of 2. Taking it at the wrong time can delay adaptation, and people with epilepsy or on warfarin need caution. Cochrane on shift work: about 24 min more daytime sleep (low quality). In a DSWPD RCT, 0.5 mg taken 1 h before the desired bedtime moved sleep onset 34 min earlier.
- **jet-lag**: supported, mainly by AASM 2007 (planned sleep schedules are an "Option" for jet lag disorder) and the pre-flight trial. The pre-flight trial suggests adding morning light to the current plain line.
- **adjust-to-night-shift-work**: mixed. The 2016 Cochrane review rates bright light and naps as low or very low quality and inconclusive. A 2025 meta-analysis found light therapy added 32.54 min of total sleep time. Evening light before night shifts helps adaptation. Naps during night shifts improve cognition (effect size 0.25); early-night naps tend to work better, and memory is briefly worse right after waking.
- **keep-naps-short-and-early**: the "short" part is supported. In cohort meta-analyses, naps of 30 min or longer were linked to higher mortality, CVD and metabolic risk, while naps under 30 min were not. In lab studies, early-afternoon naps (before 1 pm) helped cognition most. I found no direct evidence on the "before mid-afternoon" cut-off for protecting night sleep.

## New advice found
- **NEW: "Get plenty of bright light during the day, and keep nights dark."** Brown 2022 consensus and the Windred 2024 cohort (88,905 people with light sensors) support it. It broadens morning daylight to the whole day. It could replace or absorb **late-afternoon-daylight**, which has no direct evidence.
- Possible refinements to existing lines rather than new concepts:
  - Give a nap length (about 20-30 min).
  - Night-shift workers: nap early in the shift and allow about 30 min to fully wake before safety-critical tasks.
  - Jet lag: get morning light while shifting sleep earlier before an eastward trip.
  - Melatonin: the right time depends on the goal. For shifting the clock, take it in the afternoon or early evening, 2-4 h before the body's own melatonin rises (Burgess 2010). For sleep, take it near bedtime.

## Disagreements to resolve
1. **Weekend catch-up sleep.** NSF 2023 says it "may be beneficial" when weekday sleep is short. Chaput 2020 found it was associated with better health. Depner 2019 (RCT) and Chaput 2024 (device-measured cohort) found no protective effect. The myth line ("doesn't fully make up") is consistent with all of these. The "within about an hour" rule is not evidence-based.
2. **DSWPD.** The current line points to light therapy. AASM 2015 makes no recommendation for adult light therapy alone and a weak recommendation for timed melatonin.
3. **Blue-blocking glasses.** Positive narrative reviews disagree with Cochrane (very low certainty) and a 2025 meta-analysis (null).
4. **Evening light for shift workers.** Before night shifts, evening bright light is helpful (Cyr 2022), the opposite of the general "dim evening light" advice. The shift-work line should say so.
5. **Nap length cut-off.** The 2026 umbrella review suggests 60 min or less. Cohort meta-analyses point to about 25-30 min.

## Numbers to verify before they ship
- Arab 2024 (social jetlag and obesity) prints "OR 1.20; 95%CI 1.02, 1.140", which looks like a typo. This entry was dropped from the JSON but is noted here.
- Brown 2022 thresholds are melanopic EDI, not ordinary lux. Don't present them as "lux on a light meter".
- Zhao 2025 doesn't state the units for its mean differences in the abstract (probably minutes and percentage points).

## Gaps (nothing good found)
- **use-dim-red-light-at-night**: no review or RCT of red or amber night lights for sleep in healthy adults. Only the indirect Brown 2022 target of no more than 1 lux.
- **dim-lights-gradually**: no direct study. The dose-response data (Phillips 2019) is indirect.
- **late-afternoon-daylight**: no direct study.
- **fit-bedtime-to-your-body-clock**: no guideline or systematic review. The one RCT (Facer-Childs 2019) shifted night owls earlier instead of fitting bedtime to their chronotype.
- **body-temperature-low-point**: not searched in depth; it is a physiology fact, not advice.
- **AASM jet lag and shift work guidance** is still the 2007 practice parameters. I found no newer AASM update.
- Also read but left out of the JSON to keep the list focused: Gao 2025 (social jetlag and mental health, r = 0.049), Hester 2021 (blue-blocking glasses narrative review), Shechter 2018 (amber lenses for insomnia, n = 14), Schmid 2021 (smartphone filter, n = 33 men), Pan 2020 (nap dose-response, PMID 32858276), Auld 2017 (melatonin meta-analysis) and St Hilaire 2012 (light phase response curve).
