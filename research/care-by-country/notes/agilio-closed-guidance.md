# Agilio, CKS, the BNF and closed clinical guidance

Research note for the Health Culture Foundation (HCF). Compiled 2026-10-21 by a research subagent.
Rules followed: nothing invented, every claim tied to a page that was read, numbers copied exactly, and each source labelled with what was read.
No geo-block was bypassed. cks.nice.org.uk and bnf.nice.org.uk were only seen as their block pages.

## The short answer

- **Agilio Software is a UK healthcare software group owned by private equity.** Its parent company says the ultimate controllers are "certain funds advised by Five Arrows and TA Associates". August Equity founded it in 2019 and sold it in February 2024 for "a money multiple return of 9.2x".
- **Agilio bought Clarity Informatics on 26 April 2021.** Clarity is the company that writes NICE's Clinical Knowledge Summaries (CKS). Clarity began as a spin-out linked to Newcastle University and the Sowerby Centre, which built PRODIGY, the NHS-funded forerunner of CKS, in the 1990s.
- **NICE pays for CKS, but Agilio owns it.** NICE has paid Clarity to write CKS since September 2012. The last award was £1,320,000 for 2023–2026, and only one bid was received. Agilio's own page says "The content within CKS is the intellectual property of Agilio."
- **That ownership is why CKS is UK-only.** The block page and NICE's licence page both say CKS is not NICE content and is not covered by NICE's open licence.
- **The same content is sold abroad.** Outside the UK it is sold as "Agilio Prodigy" and as "Agilio: Diagnosis and Treatment Guidance" (through Pharmaceutical Press / MedicinesComplete). That second product's page says "Clinical topics commissioned and accredited by NICE".
- **The BNF works the same way.** The Royal Pharmaceutical Society, BMJ and RCPCH own it. NICE pays £15,160,000 over 12 years (2021–2033) for NHS access. NICE gave the contract without competition because the owners hold the copyright ("Direct award due to IPR ownership").
- **This is a pattern, not a one-off.** UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, DynaMed, Australia's Therapeutic Guidelines and HealthPathways are all paid or login-only. Some countries buy national licences, and those licences lapse. Norway dropped BMJ Best Practice on 1 April 2026 and deleted its translated BMJ patient leaflets at the same time. Society guidelines in the Netherlands (NHG), Japan (Minds library) and Germany (AWMF register) are free to read, though AWMF reserves its texts against data mining.

## 2. CKS: history, contract, how it is written, licensing

### From PRODIGY to CKS

- **PRODIGY (1990s).** It was built at the Sowerby Centre for Health Informatics, Newcastle University. "...the iterative design process of the PRODIGY research project, which took place between 1995 and 1998 in up to 183 general practices in the England. Release One of PRODIGY is now being rolled out to all (27,000) General Practitioners in England during 1999-2000." Source: Purves IN, Sugden B, Booth N, Sowerby M. *Proc AMIA Symp* 1999:359-63. PMID 10566381, PMC2232876 (abstract read). More PubMed records, each with metadata read and no abstract: Purves IN, "PRODIGY: implementing clinical guidance using computers", *Br J Gen Pract* 1998;48(434):1552-3, PMID 9830177. Purves I, "Prodigy, a computer assisted prescribing scheme...", *BMJ* 1996;313:1549, PMID 8978246, [doi:10.1136/bmj.313.7071.1549b](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.313.7071.1549b).
- **2006.** Connecting for Health switched the clinical evidence contract to PRODIGY. Digital Health's article "CfH confirm switch to Prodigy for clinical evidence" (Nov 2006) is behind Cloudflare and was **not read**. A search summary of it said a five-year contract went to SCHIN with EBSCO after an IP dispute with BMJ. **Unverified. Open it in Chrome.**
- **2009–2012.** The old CKS contract "came to NICE with National Library for Health (NLH)". It was re-procured in spring/summer 2012 and "Awarded to Clarity Informatics in September 2012, sister company of SCHIN, the original CKS providers". Clarity was required "to update the existing 333 CKS topics". Source: University Hospitals of Leicester library slide set, poc14_cks.pdf (full text read; an NHS library training deck, a secondary source).
- **NICE's own CKS guide** (PDF, file dated 7 July 2015, full text read) says: "In September 2012, Clarity Informatics Ltd ... was awarded a contract, commissioned and funded by NICE, to provide clinical content for a new Clinical Knowledge Summaries (CKS) service..." It adds: "The service is 100% funded by NICE, independent of the pharmaceutical industry and is provided solely by NICE to users within the UK."

### How CKS is written (NICE CKS guide, 2015 file date; full text read)

- Scope: "over 350 topics consisting of 1000 clinical presentations or patient scenarios" (2015). Agilio now says "over 375 clinical topics and 2,000 patient scenarios". Pharmaceutical Press says "370+ Clinical topics" and "1,200+ Clinical presentations". These figures disagree; see the numbers to verify.
- "More than 65 topics will be updated each year ... and up to 10 new topics will be added." "Every CKS topic is fully reviewed and updated at least every 5 years."
- Evidence: "High quality secondary evidence from NICE accredited resources (for example, NICE guidance and Cochrane systematic reviews) is identified first". Guidelines are appraised with AGREE II. "Where NICE (or other) accredited guidance has been identified ... the recommendations from the source guidance are incorporated into CKS." CKS "does not undertake a formal economic analyses".
- Review: a network of "over 6000 expert reviewers". External consultation lasts 4 weeks and "any individual may participate". Reviewers are unpaid.
- Conflicts of interest: declarations are collected annually. "Declarations of interests of CKS authors are not routinely published". Topic-level declarations appear in each topic's "How this topic was developed" section.
- Governance: an Editorial Steering Group of GPs, pharmacists, nurses, librarians and lay members. It advises when "the views of experts are strong and polarized".
- Accreditation: "The processes used to generate CKS topics have been accredited by NICE". The UHL slides say "Clarity is a NICE Accredited information provider". I did not check whether that accreditation is current; NICE's accreditation programme has since changed.

### Who may read or use CKS

- **NICE block page** (cks.nice.org.uk, HTTP 403, read 2026-10-21): "CKS is third-party content hosted on the NICE site on behalf of the publishers Clarity Informatics Ltd (trading as Agilio Software | Primary Care). It is not NICE content and is excluded from the NICE UK Open Content Licence." Access is limited to "clinicians in the NHS and to individuals within the UK ... Crown Dependencies and British Overseas Territories", for "personal or educational purposes". Organisations, including universities, need a commercial licence. "IP addresses that are not recognised as originating from the UK will be automatically blocked". "Data scraping and data mining of CKS is not permitted".
- **NICE's "CKS is only available in the UK" page** (read) says CKS "is available to users outside the UK via subscription from the Agilio | Prodigy website" and links to prodigy.clarity.co.uk.
- **Agilio's CKS page** (read): "The content within CKS is the intellectual property of Agilio." "CKS can be accessed freely by employees of the NHS, solely to benefit of the NHS and patients", students may use it for their own education, and private organisations, universities included, need "a commercial or institutional licence". No prices are shown; there is only "Request a quote".
- **Agilio Prodigy** (prodigy.clarity.co.uk, home page read): it lists about 1,141 topic links (with repeats) behind an account login. Only "Asthma" is marked "free". Sleep topics on the list: "Insomnia", "Obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome", "Restless legs syndrome" and "Sleep disorders - shift work and jet lag". I did not create an account or open the topics. Price not shown.
- **Pharmaceutical Press, "Agilio: Diagnosis and Treatment Guidance"** (read): "Covering over 370 clinical topics ... Available to customers outside of the UK." The page says "Clinical topics commissioned and accredited by NICE" and posts weekly updates (for example, 13 October 2026). Pharmaceutical Press is the publishing arm of the Royal Pharmaceutical Society, a BNF co-owner, so **the same publisher sells both UK-only resources to overseas buyers.**
- **NICE UK Open Content Licence** (read): "Clinical Knowledge Summaries and the British National Formulary are not covered by the terms of this licence." Third-party rights excluded "cover all Clinical Knowledge Summaries, the British National Formulary and its derivative outputs, the 'full' versions of NICE clinical guidelines commissioned from National Collaborating Centres, and economic models". **Even NICE's own guidance comes only with a "UK-only" reuse licence.** International reuse needs a permission form, and "Requests to use our content for artificial intelligence (AI) purposes ... are not covered".

**What this means for HCF:** the main GP-level insomnia, OSA and restless legs advice used in England was paid for with public money. Yet it is owned by a private-equity-held company, cannot be read outside the UK except by paying, and cannot be mined. That is the opposite of what the Open Health Communication Declaration asks for.

## 3. The BNF

- **Owners.** The Find a Tender notice 2022/S 000-005873 (read) states: "The BNF is owned by THE ROYAL PHARMACEUTICAL SOCIETY OF GREAT BRITAIN, BMJ PUBLISHING GROUP LTD and RCPCH PUBLICATIONS LIMITED the work that is the BNF is protected by copyright and registered to the owners".
- **NICE's national licence.** Contracts Finder b2504f68... (read) shows NICE awarded £15,160,000 for "the production and supply of the British National Formulary". The contract runs from 1 April 2021 to 31 March 2033 and went to 3 suppliers. The procedure was a "Negotiated procedure without prior publication". Find a Tender gives the reason: "Direct award due to IPR ownership". That works out to about £1.26m a year (my arithmetic).
- **Who can read it.** The bnf.nice.org.uk block page (HTTP 403, read) says: "This content is only available to NHS and members of the public (for personal reference) within the UK countries of England, Scotland and Wales. Users from countries designated as Crown Dependencies and British Overseas Territories are not permitted". It adds that private, commercial and academic bodies must license from licensing@rpharms.com, and that scraping and mining are banned. NICE's separate bnf-uk-only page (read) says "England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland" and points overseas users to MedicinesComplete. **The two NICE pages disagree about Northern Ireland.** Pharmaceutical Press (read) says NHS staff get "unlimited access" through MedicinesComplete and the BNF app, funded by "A significant investment from National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE)".
- **Why it is UK-only.** The owners hold the copyright and NICE has licensed it for UK use only. That is the same structure as CKS.
- **Open alternatives** (reachable 2026-10-21; I checked only that each site responds and what it is):
  - **emc (medicines.org.uk)**, run by Datapharm: UK SmPCs and patient leaflets, "without the need to register" (about page read). Note that Pharmaceutical Press sells emc outside the UK under licence, so even this has a commercial overseas channel.
  - **MHRA Products** (products.mhra.gov.uk): UK licence documents.
  - **EMA medicines pages**: EU product information.
  - **DailyMed (NLM)**: US labels.
  - **WHO eEML** (list.essentialmeds.org): the essential medicines list. It is a list, not a formulary.
  - None of these replaces the BNF's cross-drug prescribing advice. They are regulator labels, not guidance.
  - Australia's equivalent, the **Australian Medicines Handbook**, redirects to a login (amhonline.amh.net.au/auth), so it is closed too.

## 4. The wider pattern

See the JSON for a row per resource. In short:

- **UpToDate (Wolters Kluwer).** Subscription only. The price was not confirmed on an official page: a search summary of the Wolters Kluwer Philippines page gave "$579 / year Starting price". **Verify.**
  - Norway: Helsebiblioteket (read, published 16 March 2026) signed a new national UpToDate deal from 1 April 2026 "for tre nye år" (for three more years), giving "gratis tilgang for helsepersonell" (free access for health personnel).
  - Mongolia (from Dec 2020) and Saudi Arabia (from 2014) also have national UpToDate access, but I know this only from search summaries of vendor press releases, which were 403 when fetched.
- **BMJ Best Practice (BMJ).** Read on the US subscribe page: "$435 billed annually" for a physician, or "$39/month".
  - England: NICE procures it, as part of National Core Content, for "all NHS staff and learners in England" (BMJ, Nov 2023, read).
  - Ireland: Health Library Ireland extended a national subscription "to everyone in the Republic of Ireland" (BMJ, March 2023, read).
  - Norway: **dropped from 1 April 2026** after a tender. Helsebiblioteket also deleted all translated BMJ patient leaflets, because "Helsebiblioteket har ikke lenger rettighetene til å publisere dette innholdet" (no longer has the rights to publish this content).
- **DynaMed (EBSCO).** Subscription only. No individual price was found; the vendor pages load prices with JavaScript. Some regional consortia, such as BC's eHLbc, renew institutional licences (search summary only).
- **Therapeutic Guidelines (Australia).** Read on tg.org.au: "independent not-for-profit organisation" with "no reliance on any form of government funding or commercial interests ... relying on the Australian healthcare community to fund the guidelines through subscriptions". The price was not shown statically; a mirrored page gave $378 for 12 months. **Verify.** Some state health libraries proxy it for staff; for example, a WA Health proxy domain appeared in search results.
- **HealthPathways (Streamliners, NZ).** Regional, clinician-only. Western Victoria PHN (read): "not designed to be used by the general public and can only be accessed by using a secure login and password. There is no cost to access." It is free to clinicians but closed to the public and to other regions. The canada-oceania-latam note found that the Melbourne OSA and insomnia pathways could not be read for the same reason.
- **Map of Medicine.** Withdrawn. Pulse+IT, 4 June 2018 (read; the article is paywalled after the first paragraphs): its developers withdrew it, "with its referrals and sidebar tools withdrawn from sale on March 31 and its overall pathways map to cease on June 30". When a commercial pathway tool shuts down, the content goes with it.
- **Germany, AWMF register (with DEGAM, DGSM and others).** Guidelines are free to read, but the register is behind a bot wall (Anubis). The europe.md note records that "The AWMF register reserves its guidelines against text-and-data mining (§44b UrhG) and against AI use". The Springer journal versions are paywalled.
- **Netherlands, NHG-Standaarden.** Free to read: the europe note read the NHG "Slaapproblemen" standard as a web page. richtlijnen.nhg.org now also sits behind a bot wall (Anubis, HTTP 401 to scripts), so it is open to people but not to machines.
- **Japan, Minds Guideline Library (Japan Council for Quality Health Care).** Home page read: it publishes selected "診療ガイドライン" (clinical guidelines) and lay explanations. The asia-who note found the Japanese sleep society PDFs partly unreachable and the Japanese OSA guideline abstract-only, behind a ScienceDirect paywall.
- **China, CMA society guidelines.** The asia-who note could read only abstracts or interpretation articles for the Chinese insomnia and OSA guidelines (freeToRead false).
- **Finland, Käypä hoito (Duodecim).** HTTP 503 on 2026-10-21, both here and in the lead's Chrome check. Not read.

**The pattern:**

1. Point-of-care summaries, the tools GPs actually use, are mostly commercial: Wolters Kluwer, EBSCO, BMJ, Agilio. Even the not-for-profit ones (Therapeutic Guidelines) are paid.
2. Governments buy national licences: England (BNF, BMJ Best Practice, CKS), Ireland, Norway, Mongolia. This makes the content free at the point of use but never open. It is geo-fenced, cannot be reused or mined, and disappears when the contract changes (Norway, 2026).
3. The public pays twice: once to fund the evidence and the authors, then again to license the result back.
4. National society guidelines (NHG, AWMF, Minds, NICE guidelines) are usually free to read. But they increasingly restrict machine reuse: AWMF's TDM/AI reservation, NICE's UK-only licence and its AI exclusion, and bot walls on NHG and AWMF.

## 5. Published studies on guideline access

All checked via PubMed or OpenAlex. Abstracts read unless noted.

- **Haw et al. GUIDEMAP scoping review, dermatology guidelines.** *Br J Dermatol* 2021;185:736–744 (the citation as given in the companion piece). PMID 33937976, [doi:10.1111/bjd.20428](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjd.20428). Of 226 guidelines, "34 CPGs were behind a paywall" and "Eighty-nine CPGs did not have funding disclosures". The companion piece is "GUIDEMAP: an open-access dermatology guidelines repository", PMID 34409586, [doi:10.1111/bjd.20652](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjd.20652) (title only).
- **Anesthesia Trauma Guidelines: A Systematic Review of Global Accessibility and Quality.** *Anesth Analg* 2025. PMID 39854255, [doi:10.1213/ANE.0000000000007392](https://doi.org/10.1213/ANE.0000000000007392). Covers 165 guidelines: "While 70% were open access, the average cost for paid access was US$36.61". Only 12% had a first or last author from a low- or middle-income country. Open access was not associated with quality.
- **Uribe-Cavero et al. Clinical Evidence Behind a Paywall: An Analysis of Randomised Clinical Trials Included in Cochrane Reviews.** *Learned Publishing* 2026. [doi:10.1002/leap.2058](https://doi.org/10.1002/leap.2058); no PMID found. Of 491 RCTs, "43.0% required payment for access, ... from 60.0% for articles published between 1980 and 1999 to 20.7% for those from 2020 to 2023". This concerns the evidence base, not guidelines.
- **National licensing for the Norwegian Electronic Health Library.** *Serials* 2011. [doi:10.1629/2452](https://doi.org/10.1629/2452). Describes how Norway tendered for a point-of-care tool in 2009: "NEHL acquired national licences for BMJ Best Practice and UpToDate." Read with the 2026 Helsebiblioteket notice for the follow-on story.
- **Open to the public: paywalls and the public rationale for open access medical research publishing.** *Res Involv Engagem* 2020. PMID 32161664, [doi:10.1186/s40900-020-0182-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40900-020-0182-y). A commentary on the public's stake in removing paywalls. Supports the Declaration's argument.
- **Understanding factors influencing the use of clinical guidelines in low-income and middle-income settings: a scoping review.** *BMJ Open* 2023. PMID 37344115, [doi:10.1136/bmjopen-2022-070399](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2022-070399). Context only: access is one of several barriers.
- **UpToDate versus DynaMed: a cross-sectional study comparing the speed and accuracy of two point-of-care information tools.** *J Med Libr Assoc* 2021. PMID 34629966, [doi:10.5195/jmla.2021.1176](https://doi.org/10.5195/jmla.2021.1176). Accuracy was equal (1.35 vs 1.36 out of 2). Context on the commercial tools.

**Gap:** I found no study that measures how much primary-care point-of-care guidance (as opposed to society guidelines) is closed, or how national licences such as CKS restrict access abroad. HCF could fill that gap.
